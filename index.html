Why the lights stay on

A big power station trips. For a few seconds the grid has less supply than demand, and the only thing holding the frequency up is the spinning mass of every generator in the country. Here is what happens next, and what you can do about it.

1. The trip

Every synchronous generator is a heavy rotor locked to the grid frequency. When supply drops below demand, that stored kinetic energy is drawn down and the grid slows. The rate of fall (RoCoF) is set by how much was lost compared with how much inertia is online.

Try dropping H towards 1. This is what a grid with few spinning machines looks like.

2. Governors push back

Governors on thermal and hydro units open their valves as frequency falls. Droop says how hard: at 5% droop, a 5% frequency drop would take a unit from zero to full output. They need seconds to act, and only have so much headroom.

3. Batteries, fast

Grid batteries sense the fall and inject power within a fraction of a second, long before a governor has moved. How quickly they react matters as much as how big they are.
