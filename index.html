Why the lights stay on

A big generator trips. Supply falls below demand. Stored energy in spinning machines slows the fall in frequency while other sources respond. Change the assumptions below, then compare the model with a documented grid event.

1. The trip

Every synchronous generator is a heavy rotor locked to the grid frequency. When supply drops below demand, that stored kinetic energy is drawn down and the grid slows. The rate of fall (RoCoF) is set by how much was lost compared with how much inertia is online.

Reduce H towards 1 s. The same loss now causes a faster initial fall in frequency.

2. Governors push back

Governors on thermal and hydro units increase power as frequency falls. Droop sets the requested response. Here, 5% droop requests an extra 1% of system load for a 0.025 Hz fall. A 4 s time constant slows that response. Available headroom limits it.

3. Batteries, fast

A battery can respond faster than a turbine. Here, it uses delayed frequency feedback and reaches full output at a 0.5 Hz deviation. Reduce the delay or increase the power limit to see how the lowest frequency changes. “Battery size” means power, not stored energy.

Evidence and assumptions

4. One real trip, not a replay

On 9 October 2019, Kogan Creek in Queensland tripped from 748 MW. AEMO reports that frequency fell to 49.61 Hz. The South Australia virtual power plant detected the fall and injected power to help recovery.

Source: AEMO, Virtual Power Plant Demonstration, report #1, section 2.1.1, page 7 (PDF).

For a fixed comparison, use the same 748 MW loss on an assumed 25 GW system base: 2.992% of load. Keep H = 4 s, droop = 5%, governor time constant = 4 s, headroom = 10%, and load damping = 1. Start at 50 Hz. Omit the battery because the report does not give an aggregate battery capacity for this model.

Where the model differs

  • One frequency. The model has no transmission network, regional oscillations, voltage, or separate machines. It cannot reproduce local measurements or network constraints.
  • Assumed response. Real units have different inertia, governor settings, deadbands, and available reserves. The system base and response constants above are not measured event inputs.
  • No event battery response. AEMO documents a VPP response. This comparison omits it. Adding a guessed battery size would not establish its share of the system response.
  • No restoration or protection action. The model has no secondary frequency control, battery energy limit, or load-shedding logic. Crossing a threshold only changes the warning. The model can settle below 50 Hz.

The engineering conclusion: the model explains how power imbalance, inertia, and response affect frequency. Its higher minimum does not validate the event response. Matching one number would not prove the timing or cause. A reconstruction needs measured system conditions, response data, and a frequency time series.

Limits. A single-bus aggregate model, not an operational tool. The chart uses 49.85 Hz as the lower normal-band reference, 49.5 Hz as a single-generation-event reference, and 49.0 Hz as an illustrative load-shedding threshold. Actual limits and protection settings depend on the region and event. The model does not shed load or predict customer outages.
